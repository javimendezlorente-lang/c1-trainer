# Part 5 benchmark — Multiple-choice reading

Status: Phase 8.5A baseline

## Official format rule

Cambridge defines one text followed by six four-option questions, worth two marks each. The stated focus includes detail, opinion, attitude, tone, purpose, main idea, implication and text organisation. Questions follow the development of the text. See the [official format page](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/exams-and-tests/qualifications/advanced/format/) and [handbook](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/167804-c1-advanced-handbook.pdf).

## Observed official exemplars

The two official sample texts measured 755 and 785 running words, excluding title, instructions, questions and options. That is about 126–131 source words per question. This is an observed range, not a Cambridge-published word rule.

Both tasks sustain an argument or account long enough to test local detail and whole-text interpretation. Distractors paraphrase plausible misreadings rather than relying on visibly absurd alternatives.

## Current corpus audit

The three seed texts contain 178, 167 and 161 words—about 22% of the observed official mean. Six questions over such short passages produce a different cognitive task: answers are often direct local lookups and cannot sample the required range of organisation, attitude and implication.

Severity: **CRITICAL** under-calibration. The current items may remain regression/offline micro-practice, but they must not be presented as Cambridge-like full Part 5 tasks without revision.

## Empirically derived internal target

- 700–850 running words, normally with a developed multi-paragraph structure.
- Exactly six questions and four options each.
- Question order follows the text, while at least one item requires a whole-text view where appropriate.
- Deliberate coverage of detail plus higher-order interpretation supported by the passage.
- Options are grammatically parallel and similar in plausibility/length.

## Critic rubric and hard rejection

For every option, record the supporting or contradicting evidence span internally. Reject if the key depends on outside knowledge, another option is defensible, wording merely copies a unique phrase, a distractor is obviously irrelevant, or the source is too short to sustain Cambridge-like reading demand.
